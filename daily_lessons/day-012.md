# Day 12 — Cross-Validation

## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- Explain why one train/test split can give an unstable estimate.
- Describe k-fold cross-validation.
- Compare models using average validation error and its variability.
- Keep the final test set separate from model selection.

## 1. The core idea

A single train/test split can make a model look unusually good or bad depending on which rows land in the test set. In **k-fold cross-validation**, the training data is divided into *k* folds. The model is trained on k−1 folds and validated on the remaining fold; this repeats until every fold has served as validation data.

The average score summarizes performance across the folds. The standard deviation gives a sense of how much the score varies across those splits.

## 2. Important boundary

Cross-validation is used to compare or tune models using the training portion of the data. Keep the final test set untouched until you have selected a model. Do not use test-set results to choose features or hyperparameters.

For this small synthetic wellbeing dataset, the scores are a learning demonstration—not evidence about real patients.

## 3. Practical task

Run:

`python src/10_cross_validation.py`

The script:
1. Loads the synthetic wellbeing dataset.
2. Holds out a final test set.
3. Uses 5-fold cross-validation on the remaining training data.
4. Compares a mean baseline, linear regression, and decision trees with different depths.
5. Reports mean and standard deviation of validation RMSE, then evaluates the selected model once on the held-out test set.

## 4. How to interpret results

- Lower RMSE indicates smaller prediction errors.
- A lower mean validation RMSE is useful, but also check its standard deviation.
- Large variation across folds may mean results depend strongly on which observations are included.
- Cross-validation does not fix poor data quality, leakage, or an unrepresentative dataset.
